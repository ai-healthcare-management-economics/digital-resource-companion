# AIHE-CASE00 — Common Case-Analysis Method

## Purpose

This method provides a consistent way to analyse the companion's worked cases and locally developed institutional cases. It does not force all decisions into one score. Its purpose is to make the decision problem, evidence, assumptions, uncertainty, responsibilities, and conditions visible.

## Five-part analysis

### 1. Problem, comparator, and intervention

- Define the service problem and baseline burden.
- Identify the population, setting, decision-maker, and decision window.
- Describe the complete intervention, including data, workflow, human review, infrastructure, monitoring, and exit.
- Compare it with the best realistic alternatives, including non-technology options.

### 2. Evidence, workflow, and readiness

- Distinguish technical performance from clinical or managerial utility.
- Assess local data fitness, transportability, human factors, subgroup evidence, and response capacity.
- Identify what the organization must change for the intervention to work.
- Separate established evidence from assumptions requiring local testing.

### 3. Economics, affordability, and resource consequences

- Define the perspective, time horizon, cost boundary, and budget holder.
- Record acquisition, integration, workforce, governance, monitoring, material-change, and exit costs.
- Link claimed benefits to mechanisms and accountable owners.
- Distinguish cost-effectiveness, budget impact, affordability, and realized capacity.

### 4. Governance, distribution, and institutional risk

- Identify accountable decision rights and meaningful human oversight.
- Assess safety, equity, privacy, cybersecurity, resilience, transparency, and contestability.
- Treat mandatory safeguards independently; do not offset an unacceptable failure with a high weighted score elsewhere.
- Record affected groups, unequal benefits or burdens, dissent, and unresolved uncertainty.

### 5. Lifecycle recommendation

Conclude with one of the following: reject, redesign, validate silently, pilot, adopt conditionally, scale, restrict, pause, replace, or retire. The recommendation should identify:

- evidence supporting the decision
- assumptions and confidence
- mandatory conditions
- owners and due dates
- monitoring indicators and thresholds
- material-change triggers
- next review date
- fallback, exit, or renewal pathway.

## Writing the final recommendation

A strong recommendation is concise but auditable. It explains why the selected option is preferable to realistic alternatives, which uncertainty remains, what must happen before dependence increases, and who has authority to continue, change, or stop the intervention.
